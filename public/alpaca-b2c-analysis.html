01 · SetupDataset overview

506,892 account-month rows across 24 months, 29,794 unique accounts, nine revenue streams. The book grew 3.5× while the account base grew only 1.4× — meaning most growth came from monetizing the existing base, not from adding accounts.

Three framing facts shape everything below.

  • Revenue 3.5×, accounts 1.4×. Trailing-3-month revenue went from $362K/mo to $1.26M/mo while total accounts moved from ~18.2K to ~25.5K. The growth came from monetizing the existing base.
  • Highly concentrated book. 75% of account-months show zero revenue, and in recent months the top 1% of revenue-generating accounts drive 65–70% of revenue. The "B2C" line behaves more like an SMB book than mass-retail.
  • Two data tells. non_retail_revenue accounts for ~3% of total but spiked to 16% in Q4 2023 — odd presence in a B2C cut. And accounts flagged is_legal_entity = True generate 26% of revenue, suggesting "B2C" here means "served via the retail platform," not "individual humans."

02 · DirectionMajor trends

The book roughly tripled in two distinct phases. Through April 2024, monthly revenue moved sideways-to-slightly-up in the $325K–$520K band. From June 2024 to September 2024, revenue jumped from $634K to $1.18M — +86% in three months — and has held in the $1.0–$1.4M zone since. The March 2025 print ($1.39M) is the highest in the series.

The May 2024 wave (options PFOF + FDIC sweep) lines up with the Jul–Sep 2024 revenue inflection in the legacy streams:

  • Retail stock PFOF — $178K (Jun) → $409K (Aug) → $541K (Sep). 3× in three months.
  • Cash interest — $233K (Jun) → $402K (Aug). +73% in two months.
  • Margin interest — ~$103K plateau through summer.

Most plausible explanation. Something on the user-acquisition or activation side changed in mid-2024 — a marketing push, a tier of larger accounts onboarded, or trading activity broadened materially. The simultaneous jump in both trading-driven (stock PFOF) and balance-driven (cash interest) revenue points to net new dollars on the platform, not just higher turnover on existing AUM.

Options launch alone can't explain it: options PFOF was only $42K in Jul '24 — much smaller than the $200K+ swings in stock PFOF and cash interest. A secondary hypothesis worth noting: 2024 was a strong year for retail trading broadly, so some lift is macro tailwind that would compound any acquisition push.

05 · LinkageCorrelations between streams

I looked at this two ways. Pearson correlations on raw monthly levels are nearly all strongly positive (most pairs r > 0.7) — but that's almost entirely the shared upward trend and the simultaneous mid-2024 inflection, not genuine co-movement. The honest answer for "do these streams actually move together" comes from correlating month-over-month % changes, which strips out the shared trend.

The non-correlation worth calling out: cash interest is essentially uncorrelated with everything else on MoM changes (max |r| ≈ 0.30). It's behaving like a function of total cash balances and Fed rates, which makes it the most macro-exposed line — worth watching going into any rate-cut cycle, especially given it's still ~26% of revenue.

06 · ActionRecommendations

Sequenced by risk-adjusted priority: one defensive move that's more urgent than it looks, one low-risk growth play, and one higher-upside-but-higher-guardrail play that deserves the most operational discipline.

A · DefendBuild out cash management before rate cuts compress the cash-interest base

Opportunity

Cash interest is still 26% of revenue (~$327K/mo) and is the single most macro-exposed line in the book — when the Fed cuts, this margin compresses directly. Meanwhile, FDIC sweep launched in May 2024 and has barely moved, sitting at ~$2K/mo through Apr 2025. That's a launched-but-dormant product sitting on top of a $327K/mo revenue line that's structurally at risk. The economics are also better in sweep on a per-dollar basis through a cut cycle: sweep partnerships typically pay a more rate-durable fee/spread structure, and the higher pass-through yield is a competitive moat against Robinhood and IBKR.

Action

Two-part rollout:

  • Make sweep the visible default for new funded accounts above an idle-cash threshold (e.g., $5K), with one-click opt-out and a transparent "this is how the yield works" disclosure.
  • Run a targeted reactivation to the existing base: identify accounts with >$10K idle cash and surface a one-screen enrollment flow, emphasizing the yield delta vs leaving cash in plain interest.

Expected impact and how success would be measured

The right measurement frame is not FDIC sweep revenue in isolation — it's total cash-related revenue durability through the rate-cut cycle. Headline metrics: combined (cash interest + FDIC sweep) revenue per dollar of platform cash balance MoM; % of total platform cash sitting in sweep; net new cash inflows attributable to the higher-yield offering.

Guardrail metrics I'd hold the team to:

  • Net cash outflows. If users feel the default opt-in is shady or yields aren't actually competitive, they move money out — and the cash revenue line collapses faster than rate cuts would have caused on their own. Track net deposits and withdrawals weekly during rollout.
  • Internal yield spread vs published yield. The temptation will be to widen the spread Alpaca captures and underpay the customer. Cap the gap to a defensible floor relative to Fed Funds; if competitors close the gap, we should know before our users do.
  • Complaint volume specifically about the sweep opt-in mechanism. Defaults are legal but reputationally toxic if executed badly. Reversible-in-one-click matters.
  • Concentration risk on partner banks. FDIC limits are $250K per bank; for the top 100 accounts (71% of revenue), individual balances likely exceed that, requiring multi-bank programs. Mishandling this is a real operational/regulatory exposure.

Assumptions

FDIC sweep didn't scale because of distribution, not product issues (worth validating against engagement data the dataset doesn't include); the Fed continues cutting from here; Alpaca has bank-partnership capacity to handle the volume.

B · ExpandPush market data subscriptions deeper into the active base

Opportunity

Market data is the cleanest success in the dataset: it launched Dec 2023, scaled to ~$111K/mo within 16 months, and is the lowest-volatility stream in the entire book — it behaves like a SaaS subscription. But only ~1,740 monthly active subscribers out of 25,498 total accounts, and ~24% attach against the ~7,150 revenue-generating accounts. There's significant room before saturation, and incremental subscriber revenue is the highest-quality revenue Alpaca generates — recurring, high-margin, and uncorrelated with trading activity.

Action

Two moves: bundle a Level-2 / pro data tier into options approval and margin enrollment (the users who already self-identify as more serious); and add a free-trial trigger — 30 days of Level-2 unlocked after a user's 10th trade in a month. The product sells itself if surfaced at the right moment.

Expected impact and how success would be measured

Going from ~24% subscriber penetration of active accounts to 35% would add ~800 net subscribers; at current per-subscriber revenue of ~$64/mo, that's roughly +$50K/mo recurring — modest in absolute terms, but the lowest-risk, most rate-insensitive growth on the platform.

Track subscriber count and net adds, free-trial-to-paid conversion, monthly retention curves, and subscriber NPS.

Guardrail metrics:

  • Subscriber churn after the first paid month. If trial-to-paid conversion is high but month-2 retention is bad, we're pulling forward revenue and damaging trust — users feel tricked into a subscription they didn't notice. The Robinhood Gold playbook works only when month-2 retention holds.
  • Self-cancellation friction complaints. One click to cancel; track support tickets that mention "trying to cancel."
  • Composite ARPU on subscribers vs non-subscribers. If subscribers' trading revenue drops after subscribing (because they're now paying for data they used to get free, and trading less to compensate), the net effect could be negative. Watch combined revenue, not data revenue in isolation.
  • Options approval drop-off if bundled. If bundling data into options activation makes the options funnel feel like an upsell trap, options activation suffers too. A/B test the bundle carefully before rolling out.

Assumptions

The ~$64/subscriber/month blended figure is reasonably representative of steady-state economics (the stream is still ramping, so the truth could go either way); data-feed licensing costs scale sub-linearly with subscriber count so gross margin holds at scale; the unsubscribed active-trader segment has a real unmet need vs being satisfied with the free tier (worth validating with a small survey).

C · Grow carefullyCross-sell options to existing stock PFOF traders, conservatively

Opportunity

Options PFOF is the fastest-growing product launch in the dataset — $0 → $124K/mo in 12 months — and the highest-revenue-per-account stream we operate. But adoption is narrow: ~371 active options accounts in recent months vs ~1,580 active stock PFOF accounts, so the within-platform overlap is only ~23%. Inside the options book, 79% of revenue comes from 10 of 405 accounts, which says product-market fit is real for sophisticated users; we just haven't broadened the base. This is the highest-upside move on the list — and the one with the most ways to do damage.

Action

Run a targeted in-app activation flow for users with ≥6 months of stock trading history and a minimum balance threshold. Lead with conservative strategies (covered calls on existing positions, cash-secured puts) — not vertical spreads or 0DTE. Gate options approval behind a suitability assessment stricter than the regulatory minimum. Ship a paper-options sandbox before live trading and surface educational tooltips on Greeks and implied volatility at the strike-selection step.

Expected impact and how success would be measured

If we lift adoption among existing stock PFOF traders from ~23% to ~40% at the current per-account economics (~$335/active options account/month), that's roughly +270 net active options users and ~+$90K/mo at the 12-month mark — a ~7–8% lift on the total book.

Headline metrics: monthly active options accounts, options revenue per active account, conversion rate of stock-only users into options users.

This is the recommendation where guardrails matter most. Maximizing PFOF off inexperienced users is the textbook way to grow ARPU and destroy LTV simultaneously — exactly the failure mode to guard against. The success bar is not "+X% revenue" alone, it's "+X% revenue and options-cohort 180-day churn within Y points of the stock-only baseline and NPS not down more than Z." Specifically:

  • Churn of options-activated users at 90 and 180 days vs the stock-only cohort. Materially higher churn means the activation flow is shoving the wrong users in.
  • 90-day account drawdown distribution for newly activated options users. If the P10 user loses more than a defined % of their balance, the suitability filter is too loose.
  • NPS by tenure in options. Survey newly activated users at 30 and 90 days; a drop beyond a defined threshold vs the platform average is a real signal, not noise.
  • Support ticket and complaint volume per active options account, with a categorical lens on "didn't understand the product."
  • Concentration of trading in 0DTE / weekly contracts among new options users. If they skew toward the most lottery-ticket-like instruments, the educational content isn't working — pause the funnel.
  • Reg BI / FINRA reportable events. Non-negotiable. One serious complaint cluster can override the entire revenue case.

Assumptions

The current 405 active options accounts are mostly self-selected sophisticates, so there's genuine headroom by broadening the base rather than hitting a ceiling; conservative-strategy onboarding retains better than aggressive onboarding (well-supported by industry data but should be A/B tested in-house); regulatory and operational infrastructure exists to support tighter-than-minimum suitability without slowing the funnel to a halt.

07 · CaveatsWhat I'd verify before drawing harder conclusions

  • The non_retail_revenue column and is_legal_entity = True accounts in a B2C cut need a definition check — they may belong here, but I'd want to understand why.
  • The negative non-retail value in Apr '25 needs explanation — reversal, refund, accounting adjustment?
  • With only 12–17 live months for the new streams, correlation estimates involving them are statistically thin.
  • The dataset has no funnel data (account creation, funding, first trade), no AUM, and no rate-environment context — all of which would meaningfully sharpen the recommendations.
